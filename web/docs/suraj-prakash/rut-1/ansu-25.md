# Rut 1, Ansu 25
## The discussion at home; the Ranjit Nagara

*Dohra*

**[1]** Sri Satguru rose at first light, and washed and bathed. He went first to his mother, made his bow to her, and sat down.

*Chaupai*

**[2-3]** She gave him her blessing with love, and her heart lifted at the sight of her worthy son. She spoke to him gently, out of affection and to make him understand, for he was her one son and the love in her mind was great; and by his sitting on it the greatness is great, and the seat of the Guruship shines the more. "Listen, son. Do not do a thing that will set you fighting with the lords of the hills.

**[4-5]** "You have kept an army, and there is no wonder in that. Hunt as your mind pleases. The world worships you; make no enmity. Your saṅgat moves about you on all four sides. But I have heard now that you have had a nagārā[^1] made, and that you mean to have it sounded in the middle of the army. Sudden trouble will rise out of it. Live at peace on the bank of the Satluj.[^2]

**[6-7]** "It is not good if a quarrel falls out. Growing and growing, the suffering grows great. When the great Guru[^3] made war, he had to give up Amritsar[^4] and go out of it. There are Patshahs and kings of the earth without number. Their rule lies close at hand and their countries are great, they are able to do anything of any kind, and any friend of yours may turn enemy.

**[8-10]** "I have heard it with my own ears," she said, "that he has come near now, Bhim Chand, the great raja. He will not be able to bear the sound of the war drum, and enmity will fall between you for nothing. Have the drum sounded at your own gate,[^5] but do not ride out and make the great drum thunder. These are the great rajas, the masters of countries, and you do not come up to their equal. No ease is found in fighting, and there is always bitterness in it." Hearing his mother out, he said, "We fear no one.

**[11-13]** "We shall have our own nagārā sounded, and we shall hunt in the forest. We are seizing no one's raj; why should they make so great an enmity of it? Let Bhim Chand keep his own country and his villages secure. Why should we want war without cause? And if he takes pride in his own strength and swells the enmity for no reason, then what are we to do when it has fallen on our neck? We shall get the business done as it can be done then. Whoever carries pride in his country and his raj, Karta Purakh[^6] puts it far from him.

**[14-15]** "You tell me, 'Raja Bhim Chand is near,' and saying so you frighten me a great deal, as men raise fear in a child and tell him, 'The bogeyman is sitting there.' We shall be to the lords of the hills what they are to us: harsh with the harsh, and level with the level. We shall not begin the enmity; how should we? If they meet us we shall meet them, and if they fight we shall fight.

**[16-17]** "The Guru's house is like a mirror," he said, "always pure, purer than pure and great; and as the face that looks into it is bright, so is the fruit each man sees in it. O Mother, even as you have said it: how are we to hide, when the whole world knows us? How long are we not to have the war drum sounded? How long stay covered, and not make ourselves known?"

**[18]** He said this to his mother with the tall kalgi[^7] swaying as he spoke, then rose and came outside; and the warriors came walking up, company upon company.

**[19-20]** He went to the fine hall of assembly, where the masaṅds[^8] and the whole body of Sikhs were met, and the home of bīr ras[^9] spoke out like thunder. "Is the great drum ready, or not? Put the spices into the liquor and work them in,[^10] so that it thunders heavily. Let there be no delay now. Make it great, and bring it so."

**[21]** Then Kirpal,[^11] his mother's brother, answered him. "You know the past and the future entirely. Nothing can be said in your presence. Do what you wish, Jagannath,[^12] and do it at once.

**[22-23]** "Still, the masaṅds and the rest are light in their steadiness, and they speak in another manner with Sri Gujri. In fear they call the former wars to mind. *Thousands of warriors were broken. Close by you is a great raja, Bhim Chand, with companies of troops about him. There is no enmity between you and no alliance either, and he is to be reckoned as simply standing apart.*

**[24-27]** "Still," he said, "in statecraft the matter stands so, and wise men state it plainly: the king who is next to a king, whose country and whose subjects adjoin his own, becomes his enemy, and sooner or later, as anger rises, there is fighting; the king who lies beyond that enemy is to be made a great friend; and the third who lies beyond him stands apart. So they say. When you ride out and the war drum sounds, the rajas near at hand on all four sides will take it ill, and they will not be able to bear your glory. They will turn against you, war will fall, and warriors will die." Hearing Kirpal out, Sango[^13] said, "How then are we to stay hidden in the world?

**[28]** "The Guru's glory reaches as far as the ocean, and the lords of the hills cannot check it by any means. The Guru's house fears nothing from anyone. Sikhs come walking in from whatever quarter they will."

**[29-31]** The masaṅds standing near heard this and said, "Turn it over in your heart in every way. Weigh the enemy's force and strength against your own, and take the measure of his cunning. The world worships you; know the counsel of peace. Recognise that these are rajas, the masters of countries. And the Turks' force is greater still, and their rule runs over the four quarters. What counting is there of their cannon and their swivel guns? They are reckoned the greatest power in the world." Then Sango spoke in anger. "The Guru who was his grandfather was our own mother's father.[^14]

**[32-33]** "He gave the Turks battle, and set his hand to war many times over. This great power you speak of: he struck the enemy down and trampled them into the dust. This is that Guru's grandson. He means to increase his own glory. He has every power, he is able, and his strength is great. How will you stop him with a spoken word?"

**[34-35]** Hearing Sango's words, and the bīr ras in them, the grave Guru was well pleased. "O uncle! The lords of the hills are always near at hand. How long are we to sit still in fear of them? In our own city, or out in the forest, we shall do as our mind desires. And if they grow angry in their hearts without cause and try something of their strength upon us,

**[36-37]** "then what is it here, have we put on the body of a woman? We shall show them the edge of our swords. The command upon us is Akal Purakh's, and we speak as he inclines us. We were sent to be made manifest; why should we not do Prabhu's will? Without a war drum, how is our army, great in strength, to come by its splendour?

**[38-39]** "If we hide ourselves now and carry fear," he said, "how shall we do the rest of our work? Have it made ready without delay, and remembering Guru Nanak, have it sounded. Those hills that lie far off on the other side: we shall go as far as them, travelling at our ease, and range through many forests looking about us, and make a great hunt of it.

**[40-41]** "Bhim Chand the raja is full of pride, and all the lords of the hills follow him. On the very first day we shall let him hear it, and have the drumsticks beaten double upon the war drum. Then we shall know what he says when he hears it, and everyone's doubt will be put away." He gave his hukam to Nand Chand.[^15] "Have the great drum made ready."

**[42]** Nand Chand went and had it made ready, working the liquor and all the spices into it. It was ready, and its sound was deep; and he came into the Guru's presence and made his report.

---
*Thus in the Sri Gur Pratap Suraj Granth, in the first Rut, the twenty-fifth ansu, named the telling of the prasaṅg of "Having the Dundabhi made ready".*

*Sri Gur Pratap Suraj Granth, Bhai Santokh Singh, 1843. Gurmukhi text of record from the Sankhep Teeka edition with the annotation of Bhai Vir Singh, 1927 to 1935; the project docs abbreviate him BVS. Cite as Rut 1.25.12.*

### Notes

[^1]: The great kettledrum of a ruling house, carried on horse or elephant and beaten to announce that its lord is on the march. It was forged in the ansu before this one, and the poet calls it by several names, dundabhi, damama, dhaunsa, nishan and nagara, all of them the one drum; this file keeps nagārā where he writes it and renders the others as war drum or great drum. The Guru gives it the name Ranjit Nagara, the drum that conquers in battle, in the next ansu but one; the title of this chapter uses that name before he has bestowed it.
[^2]: The poet writes Satadrav, an older name for the Satluj.
[^3]: Guru Hargobind Sahib Ji, the sixth Guru, who kept arms and an army and fought battles, and who afterwards left Amritsar for the hills. Bhai Vir Singh identifies him here; the verse says only the great Guru. He is held up as the warning in the ansu before this one as well.
[^4]: The poet writes Sudhasar, an older name for Amritsar.
[^5]: A ruler kept a drum that was beaten at fixed hours at the gate of his house. The mother is allowing her son that drum and refusing him the other, the one that is carried out with an army on the march.
[^6]: The Creator, one of the names of God in Sikh usage. Akal Purakh and Prabhu, further on, are two more of them.
[^7]: The jewelled plume worn in the turban. Guru Gobind Singh Ji is called Kalgidhar, the wearer of the kalgi, from it.
[^8]: The agents the Guru appointed to lead the local saṅgats, gather their offerings and bring them in. Their conduct is the subject of much of Rut 3.
[^9]: The relish of heroism: the martial mood a poet raises, and the spirit that rises in a fighting man. Bhai Vir Singh reads the home of bīr ras here as the Guru himself.
[^10]: Spices worked into liquor make the dressing that is rubbed into the drum's hide, so that it will sound deep when it is beaten. Bhai Vir Singh reads the line so.
[^11]: Kirpal Chand, Mata Gujri's brother and so the Guru's maternal uncle.
[^12]: Lord of the world, a name of the divine, used here of the Guru himself.
[^13]: Sango Shah, a son of the Guru's aunt Bibi Viro and so his cousin, though the poet counts him among the nephews of the house. He falls at Bhangani in Rut 2.
[^14]: Guru Hargobind Sahib Ji was the Guru's father's father and Sango's mother's father, so the two of them descend from him on opposite sides of the house.
[^15]: Nand Chand, the Guru's minister, who carried the order for the forging of the drum in the ansu before this one.
