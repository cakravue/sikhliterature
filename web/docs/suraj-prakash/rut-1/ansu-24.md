# Rut 1, Ansu 24
## Having the Ranjit Nagara made

*Dohra*

**[1]** Raja Ratan Rai[^1] went away, having made his gift of the elephant, and the thought of the Guru left him overcome even as he went. He praised that gentle form, and his praise was great.

*Chaupai*

**[2-3]** Anandpur stood high in joy, and Guru Mukand[^2] made it bright. Saṅgats came in from wherever they were, and new companies sat down in the paṅgat[^3] every day. Gifts arrived in heaps, and open-handed men brought horses from one country after another. The Guru gave his hukam, and his servants gladly began to settle near him.

**[4-5]** They took up arms and worked at the practice of them, plying arrow, musket and sword. Men of every jāti[^4] became soldiers, and bīr ras[^5] rose high in their hearts. A good many came and took service, drawing a daily wage and receiving money in quantity, and an army of thousands gathered about the Guru, raising the cry of "Jai! Jai!" and looking on him.

**[6]** The Guru made gifts to the whole company, and knowing them for his own he was open-handed with them. He had them take sharp weapons in hand, and he gave them swift strong horses and set them galloping.

**[7-8]** In all that force there was not a war drum to be seen, and Sri Satguru turned it over in his mind. *A war drum should be had made now, and made and sounded for the spirit of the thing. Let it stay in the Guru's house always, in the time that is coming. Wherever the companies of Sikhs hold rule, over all the land of the earth as far as the ocean, let the Guru's glorious nagārā[^6] sound.*

**[9]** Having thought so in his heart, he spoke his hukam aloud. "Have a great nagārā forged. Put it in the hands of a good craftsman, one who will bring it right in every particular."

**[10-12]** Nand Chand[^7] took the order and went, and called a smith in for the forging, and told him what the Guru had said. The smith thought it over and sent for good iron, and whatever weight would be left when all the forging was done, he had four times that much weighed out.[^8] Many men set to the work at once, and being skilled they made it shapely. They got both shells ready and brought them in, and Satguru saw that they were fine and was glad. He gave out a great sum of money, and then gave the hukam for the covering.[^9]

**[13]** When the masaṅds[^10] got word of it, they turned it over together in their hearts. *Day by day he gathers a greater force, and he has set his love on arms and horses.*

**[14-16]** *Now he has had a war drum forged, and when he has it sounded in this country there is Bhim Chand, the raja of Kahlur,[^11] with a great army about him. He was away elsewhere before; his seat is at Bilaspur now, and he goes about his own country as he pleases. Word of the drum thundering will reach him. It cannot fall out otherwise, and it will go wrong: he will take the Guru for a claimant on his raj, and he will fight. We can see the trouble that will come of it, and the whole business will be spoiled.*

**[17-19]** *Living here will grow hard for us too, and those whose kingdom it is will drive us out. If war falls, great wealth will be wanted, and the whole weight of it will come down on our heads. So let us all take counsel now and see to it that he does not keep the war drum. The Guru is young in years, and being skilled he makes whatever takes his mind. Nothing can be said in his presence, and no one speaks a word against him to his face. Let us go to the mother first, and tell her the whole account, and our fear along with it.*

**[20]** Having taken such counsel, the masaṅds went to Sri Gujri[^12] in a body. They touched their foreheads to the ground, sat down beside her, and with folded hands made their ardās.[^13]

**[21-23]** "Mata Ji, hear the whole matter, and how things stand with Sri Satguru. He has gathered a force and keeps companies of them, and the warriors stay on wanting money. There is the cost of the degh[^14] for one thing, and it is heavy, for food is made ready there for men beyond counting; whoever comes, though he come three hours into the night, eats his fill from the degh and is satisfied. Then there is the warriors' half-yearly pay, and not one of them will stay without taking it. Only a man who has the rule of a country can carry an expense like that.

**[24-25]** "Sikhs and saṅgats come to his house of their own accord and offer what they are able, and nothing is taken from anyone by command, out of which the army's costs might be met. Even so there is no talk of it and no anxiety," they said. "He does not stop to consider that the income is only what it is. And now he has given the word and had a war drum forged, and it is being made ready, and everyone has seen it.

**[26-27]** "Great trouble will come of it, whenever he rides out and has it sounded. There is Bhim Chand, the raja of Kahlur, with many companies at his back. He rules a great country, and the lords of the hills follow him always. He will hear the sound of that great drum and he will not bear it, and he will reckon up his envy in his mind.

**[28-29]** "A second nagārā in his own country: how, in his pride, will he let it sound? And the Guru is young in years. He will not draw back, and he holds firm in his heart. From this we can see the mischief coming, and a war breaking out with the lords of the hills.[^15] Many troubles will rise in the course of it, and even living here will be thrown into doubt.

**[30-31]** "Counsel your son now. Stop him, and let the war drum not be sounded. The great Guru, the sixth,[^16] made war, and he had to leave his dwelling at Amritsar.[^17] Troubles of many kinds followed, and you have heard of them and seen them many times over. You know the country and you know the time.[^18] Restrain Satguru, and speak gently to him.

**[32]** "Let him take his other pleasures as his mind likes, but turn him away from what will scatter into trouble. Live at ease in your own city, and turn good counsel over in your heart."

**[33]** Sri Gujri heard the masaṅds out, and took anxiety into her heart. *They say well. Let the drum not be made. The trouble will only grow, and the house will come to grief.*

**[34-35]** She held her silence a while, and then spoke to the masaṅds with that anxiety still on her. "I shall restrain him. I shall call my son to me and tell him the prasaṅg[^19] of the sixth Guru. And as for all those who sit close about him, I shall make them understand this counsel too: *make no talk in the Sahibzada's[^20] company that gives him spirit.*

**[36-38]** "I shall call my own brother[^21] now, and have him hold them back from such talk. And the Guru's nephews,[^22] I shall make them understand: *do not set talk of war going. Go hunting, but do not go far; take his mind off it and bring him home.* By whatever means," she said, "keep him from these things. Why become troublemakers for nothing? *If he keeps warriors, let him keep them so, and let him spend as the money comes in; only let no quarrel fall out with the rajas, for the masters of these countries have heavy forces.*"

**[39-41]** The masaṅds heard the mother out and said, "Make the Satguru's son understand it in this way. *We are worshipped by the whole world, and we look on all men with an equal eye. Know your own servants on every side, and recognise enmity with no one. Keeping warriors, and a nagārā along with them, these are the great affairs of kings. Killing and being killed bring great suffering, and it is not fitting for you to earn such things.* Say this and the like, if he will take it. And forbid them all, so that none of them speaks of it in his hearing."

**[42]** Having counselled the mother so, the whole body touched their foreheads to the ground and rose. Whichever of the Guru's close Sikhs met whichever of the masaṅds, the masaṅd instructed him in the purpose he held in his heart.

**[43]** Gujri called her own brother and put it all to him: "Hold him back." He spoke to his sister and told her, "What the Guru undertakes, who is able to stop?

**[44-45]** "When men hear his purpose they honour it, every one of them, and they speak as he speaks. It is he who gives instruction to them all, and no one can say a contrary word in his presence. If the restraining of him can be done by you, then say it, and counsel him well. What power have the rest of us to speak of it? Whoever speaks, repents of it afterwards."

---
*Thus in the Sri Gur Pratap Suraj Granth, in the first Rut, the twenty-fourth ansu, named the telling of the prasaṅg of the "Dundabhi".*

*Sri Gur Pratap Suraj Granth, Bhai Santokh Singh, 1843. Gurmukhi text of record from the Sankhep Teeka edition with the annotation of Bhai Vir Singh, 1927 to 1935; the project docs abbreviate him BVS. Cite as Rut 1.24.12.*

### Notes

[^1]: The raja who gave the elephant is Ratan Rai, whose visit to Anandpur and whose gifts fill the three ansu before this one; he took his leave at the end of the last one. The verse itself calls him only the king.
[^2]: Mukand is a name of the divine, used here of the Guru himself.
[^3]: The row in which everyone sits down to eat together, without distinction of rank or birth.
[^4]: The birth-group a man belongs to. The point of the line is that men of all of them became soldiers side by side.
[^5]: The relish of heroism: the martial mood a poet raises, and the spirit that rises in a fighting man. The word comes back at Rut 1.26, where the drum doubles it in the warriors.
[^6]: The great kettledrum of a ruling house, carried on horse or elephant and beaten to announce that its lord is on the march. The poet calls it by five names in this ansu, dundabhi, damama, dhaunsa, nishana and nagara, all of them the one drum; this file keeps nagārā where he writes it and renders the others as war drum or great drum. The Guru gives it the name Ranjit Nagara, the drum that conquers in battle, two ansu further on; the title of this chapter uses that name before he has bestowed it.
[^7]: Nand Chand, the Guru's minister, who carries his orders here and appears again at Rut 3.31.
[^8]: Bhai Vir Singh's reading is that iron burns away and is reduced when it is heated and worked, so four times the finished weight had to be bought in.
[^9]: Two circular iron pieces are joined to form something like a great cauldron, and hide is then stretched over the top of it.
[^10]: The agents the Guru appointed to lead the local saṅgats, gather their offerings and bring them in. Their conduct is the subject of much of Rut 3.
[^11]: Bhim Chand of Kahlur, the hill state whose seat was Bilaspur, and the nearest of the hill rulers to Anandpur.
[^12]: Mata Gujri, the Guru's mother. The masaṅds go to her because they cannot say any of this to him.
[^13]: The petition made standing or seated with folded hands.
[^14]: The great cooking pot, and so the food cooked in it and given out to everyone who comes.
[^15]: Bhai Vir Singh gives a second reading of this line: not that the mischief is seen coming, but that, the hillmen's bad faith being known already, war with them follows from this in any case. The narrative takes the first.
[^16]: Guru Hargobind Sahib Ji, who kept arms and an army and fought battles, and who afterwards left Amritsar for the hills. The masaṅds are holding him up as the warning.
[^17]: The poet writes Sudhasar, an older name for Amritsar.
[^18]: Bhai Vir Singh reads this as two things she already knows: the country is the hills, and the hillmen are false; the time is one in which the rule is the Turks'.
[^19]: The episode, and the telling of it.
[^20]: Mata Gujri's own word here for her son, that is for Guru Gobind Singh himself.
[^21]: Kirpal Chand, Mata Gujri's brother and so the Guru's maternal uncle.
[^22]: Bhai Vir Singh reads these as Sango Shah and the other warriors of that kin. The verse itself names no one.
