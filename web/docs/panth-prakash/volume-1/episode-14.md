# Panth Prakash, Episode 14
**Questions**: 
1. Summarize Murray's point in verses [2-5], and then Rattan Singh's answer.
2. On whom did the Guru decide to bestow Patshahi (sovereignty)? 
3. What did the Guru assess about the psychology of the Sikhs, and what was the solution?

## Another telling
### (khālso hovai khud khudā: the Khalsa shall be khud khudā, his own lord)

**[1]** Then Murray[^1] said to me, "Explain it to me now piece by piece, so that I have the whole of
it: how the Satguru framed the Panth, and what else he did to make it hold.

**[2-5]** "The Turks held a great Patshahi.[^2] An order
of theirs ran at once across twenty two subas (provinces of the empire), and over the many islands in
the middle of the sea, and over the mountains of the south and the east besides. The whole country
was their subjects. How then did the Singhs spread so wide? How did their own subjects turn rebel,
and where had their army gone? Either they had no news of the Singhs; or there was no enmity then
between Turks and Gurus; or Turks and Singhs had come to some understanding; or they never knew there
was a third panth (a third faith beside Hinduism and Islam). Or there was no shrewd man among them,
so that they never made out the Singhs as they came up. Or the Panth never raised a claim to
Patshahi. Or the Panth was made where they could not see it."

**[6]** Hear now the answer I gave him, Gursikhs. This Panth was never hidden from the day it was
born: like dabh grass (the sharp grass spread for rites) and thorns, it was sharp at the point from
the first.

**[7-9]** When was the Singhs' Panth ever got up in hiding? When could the Singhs' Panth be kept out
of sight? The Singhs' Panth was made for strife, and the Singhs were born with weapons in their
hands. The Singhs were given the pāhul of the khanda, and the khanda was their gurhtī.[^3] They wear
quoits and daggers on their heads. When could they hide themselves, any more than a lion hides its
claws? So the perfect Satguru framed the Panth, and framed it for strife, because that was what had
to be done. When was Patshahi ever got without war? From the beginning the Guru raised up the way of
war.

**[10-12]** Between the Gurus and the Patshah the enmity had grown great. Guru Tegh Bahadur (his
father, the ninth Guru) had given his head; how were his Sikhs to wish them well? Guru Tegh Bahadur
laid his body at Delhi's door, and with it he tore up the root of the lord of Delhi. Guru Gobind
Singh saw it plainly: the Turks' root had now dried. But a tree does not fall for being cut unless it
is ripe, or unless a great wind comes and throws it. *So now let the wind of the khanda blow upon
them; strike them that way and bring them down.*

**[13-15]** At that the Satguru took his own sword in his hand. *Now kill the Turks and take the
Patshahi.* Then he turned another thought over. *What have we to do with a worthless Patshahi? We
hold Sri Nanak's seat in our house, and Patshahi has fallen at its feet. What care have we for
Patshahi? Let us raise up our own servants and give it to them.* The Satguru settled it in his mind,
that the rule of these rajas must be overturned, and that all these hill rajas should be given the
khanda to hold.

**[16-18]** Then it came clear to him. *When were these ever going to become our Sikhs? Why give them
a bounty for nothing? Why kiss the mouths of other men's sons? These are stone-worshippers, and water
does not soak them; from the first they have been ungrateful at the Guru's door. They call themselves
Rajputs and soldiers in their own right, and they will never say the Guru gave them Patshahi. Now let
the Patshahi be given to the poor and let them be raised up. They will know that the Guru gave it as
a mother gives.*

**[19]** *A thing should be given to a poor man; what is the good of giving where there is much
already? This is the house of the Guru, which is compassionate, and it is called the cherisher of the
poor.*

**[20-23]** *The seven sanats and the twelve castes,[^4] who know nothing of the business of rule;
those the world calls Jatts and rustics; those called banias and shopkeepers, kirars and Khatris.
Blacksmiths and carpenters, who were low castes; calico-printers and distillers, and on these low
ones he had mercy. Gujjars, cowherds and Ahirs, counted base-born; Kambojs and Sudras, whom nobody
asked after. Water-carriers, barbers, Arorias and potters; Sainis, goldsmiths, sweepers and
leather-workers. Bhatts, and Brahmins who lived by asking; travelling players, Labanas and potters
again. On these poor ones we shall bestow the Patshahi, and they will keep our Guruship in mind.* So
the Satguru called the Sikhs and challenged them: take up weapons and go kill Turks.

**[24-27]** But the poor Sikhs would not agree to it, being in dread of the Turks. "Their armies are
past counting; when would they ever let go of rule and land?" So they would not accept what he said.
"When were Turks ever to be killed by us? We are sparrows and they are trained hawks; we are kids and
they are wolves in array. How will you have deer kill lions? You are making the water of the low
ground climb the ridge. They are soldiers from the first, Mughals and Pathans; we are Jatts and
rustics, barbers and carpenters. You have picked out the low castes, every one of them without a
weapon on him. Call out some Rajput king instead; he is the one who will do your work for you."

**[28]** Then Sri Satguru grieved. *What is this the Sikhs have done? We wished to give them
Patshahi, and they have said no, and no again.*

**[29-30]** Sri Guru was the knower of hearts, and he made out the whole of it. This charan
pāhul[^5] is of a peaceful form; there is nothing fierce in it at all. They wear the seli topi (the
woollen cap of the older Sikhs) on their heads and call themselves servants. Their form is all
compassion, and weapons cannot be put into hands like that.

**[31-34]** *Now let the Sikhs' form be turned about*, the Satguru said within himself, *and let them
be made so fierce that a man takes fright at the sight of them. Let some name of fierceness be given
them, and let pāhul be made and fierceness poured into them.* So he took it into his mind, and made
the effort after thought: let them take the beautiful form of a Kshatri (the warrior caste), keep the
hair of their heads and bind turbans upon them. Singh was a name that belonged to the Kshatris, and
Sri Guru saw that this was the best of them. He settled the khande kī pāhul in his mind: in this way
the Khalsa will be unbending. So they will learn Patshahi and lose what they have been; at every
saddle a Patshah.[^6] From reeds and chaff they will be beaten into iron, and having drunk the pāhul
of the khanda they will not turn back from the khanda.

**[35]** The Khalsa shall be khud khudā,[^7] his own lord, as all excellence is the excellence of
God. He shall take no other's word for anything, save the one true Patshah's.

**[36]** He shall not hold with ghosts and spirits, nor with grave-shrines and cremation grounds, nor
with Gugga Sultan.[^8] He shall not wear the tilak (the mark on the forehead) or the janeū (the
sacred thread of the twice-born), and shall not put on the dhoti; they shall all eat from one bowl
and let go of gotra (the clan line a family reckons itself by).

---
*Thus in the Sri Gur Panth Prakash, the fourteenth sakhi, another telling.*

*Sri Gur Panth Prakash, Rattan Singh Bhangu, 1841. Gurmukhi text of record from the edition of the
Institute of Sikh Studies, Chandigarh, volume 1, episodes 1 to 81, with the English translation of
Kulwant Singh. Punjabi commentary from the Prachin Panth Prakash Steek, part 1, of Singh Sahib Baba
Santa Singh Akali, Jathedar of the 96 Krori Budha Dal. Both commentators are named in full in the
notes; the project docs abbreviate them KS and BSS. Cite as Panth Prakash 14.9.*

### Notes

[^1]: Captain David Murray, the English officer at Ludhiana at whose asking Bhangu told this whole granth; episode 2 tells how that came about. The poet writes the name both as Mari and as Mali.
[^2]: Patshahi is the rule itself, the thing a Patshah holds; Patshah is the holder of supreme rule, a word the text uses of the Gurus and of the Mughals alike.
[^3]: pāhul: the draught by which a Sikh is initiated, here the khande kī pāhul, prepared with the khanda, the double-edged sword; episode 15 gives the ceremony. · gurhtī: the first sweet thing put into a newborn's mouth, which is held to shape the person for life. The word is kept because the line turns on it: the Singhs took theirs from the steel.
[^4]: The seven sanats and the twelve castes is a set phrase for the whole range of birth-groups below the twice-born. Of the communities named here, the Jatts are the land-owning peasantry and the largest in Punjab; banias and kirars are trading and money-lending groups, and Khatris the mercantile caste the Gurus came from; Gujjars and Ahirs herd cattle, Kambojs cultivate, Arorias trade in the west Punjab and Sainis are market gardeners; Bhatts are hereditary bards and Labanas a carrying and trading community; Sudras are the fourth and lowest of the four varnas, and sweepers and leather-workers fall outside the four altogether.
[^5]: The older initiation, in which a Sikh drank water touched by the Guru's toe. Guru Gobind Singh put it aside for the khande kī pāhul, and Baba Santa Singh Akali sets the two side by side exactly as the verse does, the one peaceful and the other carrying power.
[^6]: At every saddle a Patshah renders haṅne haṅne, which becomes the standing Khalsa claim that every Singh in the saddle holds Patshahi in his own right.
[^7]: khud khudā: his own lord, from the Persian khud, self, and khudā, God. The source prints the phrase as this episode's tagline, and the whole couplet is built on the run of khu- sounds through it. It is kept because it is the point the argument has been travelling toward and because no English phrase carries both halves.
[^8]: A saint whose shrines drew Hindu and Muslim worshippers alike, also called Sakhi Sarwar. His followers are the Sultanis of episode 16.
