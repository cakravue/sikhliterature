# Panth Prakash, Episode 2
## Now the telling of the second sakhi is written
### (jim utpati bhaī khālse: how the Khalsa came into being)

When the Patshah spoke to them in this way, that no one had ever given these people a country, that they were subjects of his who had turned rebel while Nadir and Ahmad Shah were coming down on the land,[^1] the English wrote it down just as he said it: no one gave the Singhs a country, the Singhs hold no parwana, and no Patshah ever made them a grant.[^2] As the Shah told it, so it went into the book. They had their orders and they set it down in haste, that the Singhs held no parwana and that no Patshah had granted them anything.[^3] How long can rebel subjects be left alone? The country would be taken off them. And when the English had written it down so, the wicked Mughal was filled with delight.[^4]

Why should a Turk not rejoice, and why should he hold back from a crooked word? Between Turks and Hindus stood the enmity of dīn and mazhab, and it went for the throat.[^5]

Several years passed in this way. Then Ranjit Singh began to harry the chiefs who held the small states east of the river, and they brought the English in and seated them at Ludhiana.[^6] In Sammat 1864 Bikrami (1807 CE) the English put Ranjit Singh's army back across the Satluj.[^7] Terms were settled and accepted, that they would not so much as look across the Satluj to the other side. They gave the chiefs their assurance, and wrote their country down as protected.[^8]

General Ochterlony kept the matter in his mind. He came to Ludhiana, and once he had come among the Singhs he began asking about it.[^9] The thought turned over in the general's mind of that account the Patshah had had written down. Under him was Captain Murray, a man who knew a great many people in the country.[^10] The general sent for him, sat him down beside him, and explained it all to him thoroughly: have the origin of the Singhs written out, and how the Khalsa first came by a country.[^11]

"See whether it stands as the lord of Delhi told it, or whether the Mughals have worked some mischief into it. There is no agreement at all between Hindu and Turk, and when was a Musalman ever going to tell the truth?[^12] How did Jats overthrow the Shah's governors? How did a Shah come to be beaten by his own subjects? The Mughals held twenty two provinces, and many islands in the sea besides.[^13] All Hind was in the hands of the Chughtais. What outrage was it that destroyed them? The subjects are goats and the ruler a lion; the ruler is a hawk and the subjects quail.[^14]

"How did goats kill lions, how did quail kill hawks, and how did subjects kill their rulers? Nothing but a miracle does work of that kind.[^15] Either there is some power and strength in these people, or they are the panth of someone mighty. Write out the whole truth of them, and then we shall come to the bottom of it."[^16]

Murray told the general to write it as the Shah of Delhi had told it, and to send it so to the King in London.[^17]

The English general was a man of great learning, and he would not accept what Murray said. If the Patshah's account should turn out false, it would make a liar of him and of everything he sent.[^18] So he turned a second thought over, being old and very watchful: between the Singhs and the Patshahs the general saw a sharīkā.[^19]

"If what the Shah said is true, all those people will bear witness to it. If the Shah has spoken a lie, then sift the true from the false.[^20] Or let some old writing be found; that way my mind will be satisfied. Send that writing to London, and let there not be one sesame seed of falsehood in it.[^21]

"Have it written down in every particular, how the Singhs took possession of a country, and in what years. All the world has heard the quarrel of the Khalsa and knows the account of it."[^22]

Murray took the order and set to work. He sent for Maulvi Butay Shah and commanded him to write the book, and to write out the origin of the Singhs.[^23] "Write me only what is true, and mix no falsehood into it. This goes to the King in London, and it will be published in the other continents as well."[^24] With warnings of this kind he made it plain to Butay Shah: write the truth of the Khalsa, and how the Singhs grew and increased.[^25]

I had gone to that court myself, and hearing this I grew troubled. This maulvi is a Musalman; when was he going to speak the Sikhs' praise?[^26] I talked it over with Murray and brought him round, and he showed me what the man had written. When I had read it I spoke out, that he had not told the whole of the Khalsa's matter.[^27]

So Murray became a friend of mine, and he called me to him and said, "Have it written down as the Khalsa came into being."[^28]

Then I said, "Listen, Captain Murray. You are putting your trust in a Musalman. Between Turks and Hindus there has been enmity from the beginning, and they keep a quarrel going, striking and striking back.[^29] Turks speak crooked of Hindus, and Hindus go on abusing Turks." Then Captain Murray answered, "We English are shrewd men and knowing.[^30] We weigh the true and the false well, as a swan separates milk from water. Have the truth written for us and have no misgiving; if it is false we shall not write it down.[^31] Wherever we English go into another land, we write out the whole account of that place and carry it back with us, and whatever is true and of note we send to our King in London."[^32]

Then Murray said to me, "Tell me just this much. How did the Singhs come by a country, and which king gave it to them?"[^33]

I answered him so: "The true Shah gave the Singhs their Patshahi." Murray said, "And who is this true Shah?" We said, "Shah Nanak is he."[^34] Murray said, "Nanak was a faqir. What did he know of the management of a kingdom?" We said, "He is the Shah of Shahs, the true Patshah of dīn and of dunī.[^35]

"Many a Shah he made a faqir, and many a faqir he made a pir, and he himself stayed beyond all caring. So Nanak became the Shah of Shahs.[^36] Whoever touched Shah Nanak's feet, so much power came into them that sparrows killed hawks at their bidding and goats tore lions apart.[^37]

"Men who had a hundred thousand horse at their heels and cannon by the thousand, who sat upon thrones and held Patshahi: his servants brought them to ruin.[^38] His servants take a nugdā of bhang and take the Nām, and fling it at a man's head, and he is left with no power to stand, and is destroyed on the spot."[^39]

Then Murray spoke to me with affection: "Tell me by what way Guru Nanak ji came by his Shahi." I told it to him as I tell it to you now, saints and friends and brothers. God called him near and made it plain to him with his own mouth. "I have made you a raja on my own behalf; whose strength can come near it?" He pressed him to his own body as he said it. "Go, and go now. Let your name be called Nanak, and go and take birth in the Bedi lineage. Keep whoever comes into your refuge, and crush the wicked down."[^40]

Murray said, "Tell me the whole occasion of it, how God sent him that kingship." The answer I gave him, hear it now, you discerning saints.[^41]

In Kaliyuga a great strife had fallen. Unrighteousness grew and dharam weakened. Kings were caught in the greed of māyā, and rulers had thrown their own dharam down a well.[^42] Those who had founded panths and come into the world, their panths too had slipped away from them. The love of māyā had cheated them all: they lost their dharam, and wanted nothing but to fill their own lineage.[^43]

---
*Thus in the Sri Gur Panth Prakash, the second sakhi, the telling of how the Khalsa came into being.*

*Sri Gur Panth Prakash, Rattan Singh Bhangu, 1841. Gurmukhi text of record from the edition of the Institute of Sikh Studies, Chandigarh, volume 1, episodes 1 to 81, with the English translation of Kulwant Singh. Punjabi commentary from the Prachin Panth Prakash Steek, part 1, of Singh Sahib Baba Santa Singh Akali, Jathedar of the 96 Krori Budha Dal. Both commentators are named in full in the notes; abbreviated them KS and BSS. Cite as Panth Prakash 2.14.*

### Notes

[^1]: Patshah: a king, a sovereign. Bhangu uses it both of the Mughal ruler at Delhi and, as Sikh usage does, of the Gurus. · Nadir Shah came out of Persia and sacked Delhi in 1739. Ahmad Shah Abdali invaded Punjab eight times between 1747 and 1767, and destroyed Harmandir Sahib in 1761.
[^2]: parwana: a written order or warrant; here the written authority by which a Patshah makes a country over to a subject. It is the only ground on which the English would recognise a claim.
[^3]: Shah: the same word as Patshah, short.
[^4]:
[^5]: Turk is Bhangu's word for a Muslim, whatever his origin, and not for a native of Turkey. · dīn and mazhab: faith and creed, the pair of Persian words in which the quarrel of religions was conducted in the courts of the time. The accent on dīn is there only to keep it apart from the English word.
[^6]: The chiefs are the Sikh houses east of the Satluj, outside Ranjit Singh's country: Patiala, Nabha, Jind, Kaithal and their neighbours, the Phulkian states. Ranjit Singh's forces crossed into their territory often enough that they feared for their rule, and they asked the English for protection against him. Baba Santa Singh Akali's reading is that the fault was Ranjit Singh's from beginning to end: these were Sikhs of his own Panth and subjects of no one, and because he meant to take their country by force rather than leave them to rule it, the English came in at a door he had opened himself.
[^7]: Kulwant Singh's reading is that there was no fighting at all: Ranjit Singh made a few excursions south of the Satluj, brought the Phulkian rajas to terms, took their tribute and went home, and the crossings stopped after the treaty of 1809.
[^8]: The settlement is the treaty of 1809, which made the Satluj the boundary between the English and Ranjit Singh's country and put the states east of it under English protection.
[^9]: The poet writes the name as Loni Akhtar. Kulwant Singh: "Sir David Ochterlony, An American by descent, who was a commander of the British forces in Bengal. His forces under the overall command of Lord Lake defeated the Maratha Army under the command of Jaswant Rao Holkar and captured Delhi in 1804. Later on, he was the British representative at Delhi dealing with the British relations with the Sikh State of Maharaja Ranjit Singh."
[^10]: The poet writes the name in two forms, Mari and Mali. Kulwant Singh: "Captain Murray: An official of the East India company or the Britishers posted as a political agent and a subordinate of Sir Ochterlony at Ludhiana. He got the history of the Sikhs recorded from Butay Shah Maulvi and Rattan Singh Bhangoo."
[^11]:
[^12]:
[^13]: Jats are the land-owning peasantry, the largest community in Punjab, and they made up the bulk of the Khalsa misls and later of Ranjit Singh's army.
[^14]: The Chughtais are the Mughals, named for Chaghatai, the son of Genghis Khan, from whose line Babur came. · Baba Santa Singh Akali answers the general's question here with a tradition of Guru Gobind Singh: when the news of the younger Sahibzade reached him he was sitting cutting at a clump of grass, and he cut its root through and said that he had now cut the root of the Mughals, and that they would never grow green again.
[^15]:
[^16]: panth: a way, and so a following or an order founded by a teacher; in Sikh use, the Khalsa Panth.
[^17]:
[^18]:
[^19]: sharīkā: the rivalry between kinsmen who share a single inheritance, the ordinary Punjabi word for a feud between co-heirs. It is kept here because no English word carries the kinship and the enmity at once, and it is the relation Bhangu says the general saw.
[^20]:
[^21]:
[^22]:
[^23]: maulvi: a Muslim scholar of religion and law. · Kulwant Singh: "Maulvi Butay Shah alias Ghulam-Muhayy-ud-Din, a Muslim scribe and an official in Captain Murray's office whose services were summoned by David Murray to write history of the Sikhs." In Baba Santa Singh Akali's account he was a learned man, and lived at Ludhiana.

[^31]: The swan of Indic tradition can take the milk out of a mixture of milk and water and leave the water behind.
[^32]:

[^34]: Patshahi: the kingship itself, the thing a Patshah holds.
[^35]: faqir: a religious mendicant, one who has given the world up. · dunī: the world, as against dīn; the two together are the whole of what a Patshah holds.
[^36]: pir: a Muslim spiritual master, the head of an order. · Baba Santa Singh Akali sets beside this line the words of Gurbani, *nānak pātisāhī pātisāhu* (Nanak, the Patshah of Patshahis), which close the twenty fifth pauri of Japji Sahib.
[^37]:
[^38]:
[^39]: nugdā: a pellet of bhang, ground and rolled in the hand to be swallowed. · bhang: a preparation of cannabis. · Nām: the divine Name. · Baba Santa Singh Akali reads the line as a boast about the servants rather than the master: a Singh flings the pellet as another man fires a cannonball, and the men who do nothing grander than scour the vessels of the degh in the Guru's langar have power enough that no cannon is wanted.
[^40]: Kulwant Singh: "Bedi Kul or Bedi Dynasty: The House of Bedis, a Caste name among the Kashtriyas inhabiting in this region. Guru Nanak was born in a Bedi family at Rai Bhoi ki Talwandi in District Sheikhupura now in Pakistan."
[^41]:
[^42]: Kaliyuga: the last and worst of the four ages of Indic time, and the age the world is now in. · dharam: righteous duty, the settled order of right conduct. · māyā: worldly illusion, and the wealth and power that carry it.
[^43]: Baba Santa Singh Akali names the founders of the earlier panths as Ramanand, Dattatreya, Gorakh and Machhindra, on the authority of the Sarbloh Granth. Kulwant Singh reads the line as pointing to the ascetic orders of Gorakh Nath and his followers, in the time before Guru Nanak.
