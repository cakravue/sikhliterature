# Panth Prakash, Episode 16

## The sakhi of the spreading of the Khalsa Panth
### (im khālsai paṅth bhayo utpaṅn: so the Khalsa Panth came into being)

**[1]** From that day the Khalsa began to spread. Five, then fifties, then hundreds joined, and
Singhs stood up every day.[^1]

**[2-3]** So the Satguru gave all the work to the Khalsa and made the Khalsa his agent in every
place. To the south, the east and the north he sent them, and to the west and the middle country too.
To Amritsar and to Patna, and wherever the Guru had a seat of his own, he sent bhujangis[^2] with full
authority, giving them assurance and taking thought for them.

**[4-6]** He gave leave at every seat of the Guru that the pāhul of the khanda (the draught of
initiation, prepared with the double-edged sword) might be given there on their own account.
*Wherever five bhujangis are, take it as equal to a gurdwara. Wherever five bhujangis come together,
without question let them make the pāhul and give it to drink. Have the ardās (the standing petition
made before the saṅgat) done by the five bhujangis, and whatever you ask for, that is the fruit you
will get. What is offered at the gurdwaras, let the Singhs themselves eat; what is left over, send it
on to me.* And he sent letters out to the saṅgats: come and take the pāhul of the khanda.

**[7]** *Drink the pāhul of the khanda and be made fierce, and now take the killing of the Turks
in hand. Power has come into the pāhul of the khanda: the
Turks will run, and the Panth will stand.*

**[8]** What the Satguru said, the Majhails (the Sikhs of the Majha, the country round Amritsar) took
to heart. And others too, whoever took it to heart, their fortune became a blessed one.

**[9-10]** So the Khalsa Panth came into being, and the Satguru was greatly pleased to see it. He put
weapons into the Khalsa's hands and had them wear dress of every kind. He would sit in the middle
with a cot set down for him, and the Singhs stood all around him with their weapons on. As Kanh sits
among the gopis, Krishna among the cowherd women, so the Satguru shone among the Singhs.

**[11-13]** He had them drill and run, and sometimes stood them all round and looked them over. Some
he seated and made others rise; some he made get up, and some he set running. To some he gave heavy
staves, and they drilled in pairs. Sometimes he would go and stand in a ploughed field, and the
Singhs would pick up clods and fight with them. They played sauṅchī (a village game of holds and
grappling), gripping and wrestling, and the Satguru was delighted to watch it, and would settle
countries and jagirs (grants of revenue land) on them.

**[14-15]** "Ask for jagirs with your own mouths, brave men," he said, "and the country shall come to
you. Wherever your mind is satisfied, that is what I shall settle on you." Hearing it, they fell
close at hand: they should like to take the Punjab now. The Satguru said, "Ask for the good country
of the south; ask for the hills, and the east and the west."

**[16-19]** The Singhs said, why should we go far off? We shall rule in the middle of Punjab. Again
and again they asked for Punjab, though they would be ruined fighting one another. The Satguru said,
ask for a wide jagir; take in as much of it as you please. But the Singhs burn themselves up in
sharīkā,[^3] and say that they will have their score out of their own. They wanted lands near their
own houses, close at hand, and would take in only that much country. They were the sons of poor men,
and so could not make a long reach. Kinsman beside kinsman, near beside near, they asked for land
next to their Punjab. The Satguru would have given them foreign countries, and they had no wish for
them.

**[20]** Sri Satguru told them plainly: you will squabble and squabble and stay in Punjab. But
whichever Singh came along behind, to him the Satguru named a country elsewhere.

**[21-24]** Much of this is written at length in the Gur Bilas,[^4] and I set it down only in brief.
What Sukha Singh kept back, I bring out and say. Whoever wants the whole of it, let him go and look
in the Gur Bilas: how the hill chiefs made war, and how the evil of it came back on their own heads.
He has said much of it there; I tell what was left over from him. This telling is vast beyond all
measure, and when shall I ever reach its far side? I shall enlarge it only so far as my understanding
will reach. It has now come round to the Singhs; hear it, beloved gurmukh brothers.

**[25]** Then the Satguru gave his command, making it plain to the Majhails: first increase the
Khalsa, and after that set the strife going.

**[26-27]** First he sent bhujangis toward Majha, and by giving pāhul they settled Guru ka Chak,
which is Amritsar. So the Panth of bhujangs and Nihangs[^5] grew great, with staves in their hands
and khandas hung at their necks. Some went about, some stayed in gurdwaras; some built dharamsals
(halls kept for assembly and for travellers) and some raised bungas (lodges beside a shrine). Bhujangis invited bhujangis and fed them, and
kept no company with anyone else.

**[28-30]** If a bhujangi was in real need of a thing, he would look about and take it from a Singh's
house. He would lift just so much stuff as he wanted, the ghee and the flour and the salt he was
going to eat. He would call out from the door that the Nihangs had come, and the Sikh woman would
open it without hesitating. Being a Sikh woman she would do as she was told, and put out as much as
he wanted. So Singhs came and stood every day, asking about and seeking out the Singhs' houses, and
the Sikh women ran to give to them and spoke kindly as they gave.

**[31-32]** The Panth had grown very large, and the cost of it grew large with it. Whatever the poor
Singhs had not got at home, they lifted from the houses of others. They lifted food and clothing, and
pushed silver and gold away. But when they took to demanding on one side and the other, in many
places there was a brawl.

**[33-35]** There were a great many Sultani[^6] people in those parts then, and they kept up much
quarrelling with the Singhs. If there was a Singh's household among them, they wanted it driven out.
It is for your sake the Singhs come to our houses, they told that household, and they harassed it
hard. So the enmity of the Sultanis and the Singhs grew, and a brawl broke out between them. In the
houses of the Guru's Singhs there was want, and in the Sultanis' plenty. They would not let the
Khalsa in, so the Khalsa went in at odd hours.

**[36-39]** Where a few Singhs were not let in, the Singhs were sent outside the village. And when
the bhujangis made up a large jatha (a band), ten or twenty together would make a camp. They would go and
pitch it beside a village that had troubled them much. If it would not give, they harried it hard,
and laid waste its wells and its fields. In this way the Khalsa took hold, and some days passed so.
The Panth grew greater still, and the Singhs settled on taking nazrānā.[^7] Day by day the Panth
grew, and food and clothing ran short. So the Panth passed a resolution: a nazrānā must be laid on
the villages.

**[40]** When people would not pay the nazrānā, the Singhs raised a brawl and took it. So they went
to the Turks and laid a complaint before them.

**[41-43]** Then a Turk force came up and took a great many Singhs. They caught them and sent them
off to Lahore, and the Turk nawab (the Mughal governor of the suba) had them put to death. When this
was done to the Singhs, the Singhs too set up an uproar. Seeing a few, the Singhs would kill them;
seeing many, the Singhs went off into the scrub. So the quarrel between the Singhs and the Turks
grew. When had there ever been any sweetness between Singhs and Turks? Dishonest Hindus took the
Turks' side, and the Panth's enmity grew the greater.

**[44-45]** The Sultanis were half Musalman, and called themselves Hindus, false and good for
nothing. They pointed the Singhs out and had them seized, so the Singhs sought them out at home and
killed them. Whatever Singh came into their hands, they killed him where he stood. So the feud
between the Singhs and the Sultanis grew very great.

**[46-49]** When a few of them could not get hold of the Jatt Singhs (the land-owning peasantry, who
were the bulk of the Panth), they brought Turks up in numbers. Then the Singhs went and sat together
in the scrub, and fell on the Turks front and rear and stripped them. When their strength would not
reach the Panth, they seized the Singhs' fathers and mothers and hunted them down. And other Singhs
who were working for their living, they caught and had killed. Singhs who stayed in gurdwaras, who
sat in dharamsals and bungas, they gave up to the Turks as well, and the Turks struck their heads
off. Wherever else a Singh came to hand, they had him killed there and then. Even so the Singhs did
not give over what they were doing; a Majhail had no fear of dying.

**[50]** Their discipline was excellent and their understanding excellent, and excellently they
taught the sacred verse. Men gave up their place under a ruler and in the revenue office, and came and joined the
Satguru's Panth.

**[51-53]** Day and night the Panth went on increasing, and many brought food for them from their own
houses. One man himself, another's son or another's grandson, would see its great glory and join the
Khalsa. Whoever was hungry, whoever was naked, joining the Khalsa he found ease. Whoever was in
arrears on his revenue, whoever had taken a loss in his trade, whoever had had force laid on his neck
and could find nowhere to live wherever he went, such a man went into the Khalsa Panth, and had no
more grief of anyone.

**[54]** In this way the Khalsa grew every day and increased from one generation to the next. That
Panth is as unmoving as the ocean; who can dry it up?

**[55-56]** The Panth was still few and its equipment few, so it could not get its hands on power.
The whole country was with the Turks, who were many and kept a great standing army. Great forts they
had and great artillery, and the Singhs had nothing to set against them. The Sultanis joined with
them and made trouble, and the Singhs left their homes and went off into the scrub.

---
*Thus in the Sri Gur Panth Prakash, the sixteenth sakhi, the telling of the spreading of the Khalsa
Panth.*

*Sri Gur Panth Prakash, Rattan Singh Bhangu, 1841. Gurmukhi text of record from the edition of the
Institute of Sikh Studies, Chandigarh, volume 1, episodes 1 to 81, with the English translation of
Kulwant Singh. Punjabi commentary from the Prachin Panth Prakash Steek, part 1, of Singh Sahib Baba
Santa Singh Akali, Jathedar of the 96 Krori Budha Dal. Both commentators are named in full in the
notes; the project docs abbreviate them KS and BSS. Cite as Panth Prakash 16.14.*

### Notes

[^1]: Baba Santa Singh Akali names the next five to be initiated after the Panj Piare, who are remembered as the Panj Mukte: Deva Singh, Bhagwan Singh, Tahil Singh, Ishar Singh and Fateh Singh.
[^2]: bhujaṅgī: literally a young snake, and so a young Singh, sharp from birth. It is Bhangu's standing word for the Guru's picked men, and "young Singh" loses the image the word is carrying. · Baba Santa Singh Akali says it was Bhai Mani Singh who was sent to Amritsar, and that the letters of the verse below were written out by the Guru's scribes and sent as far as Dhaka and Bengal.
[^3]: sharīkā: the rivalry between kinsmen who share a single inheritance. It is kept because no English word carries the kinship and the enmity at once, and the relation is the whole reason the Singhs ask for land next to their own.
[^4]: The Gurbilas Patshahi 10 of Bhai Sukha Singh, granthi of Kesgarh Sahib, written in Sammat 1854 Bikrami (1797 CE), some forty years before this granth. Baba Santa Singh Akali reads the line as Bhangu saying he tells what Sukha Singh held back, rather than repeating what Sukha Singh set down.
[^5]: Nihangs are the Singhs of the armed order that keeps the blue dress and the arms of this period. In Baba Santa Singh Akali's gloss bhujaṅg is the young snake that stings and nihaṅg is the fighter, and the two words are used here of the same men.
[^6]: Followers of the Muslim pir Sakhi Sarwar, also called Gugga Sultan, whose shrines drew Hindus and Muslims alike. Baba Santa Singh Akali describes them going village to village making disciples and setting people to worship graves and spirits. They are what a Singh is forbidden to hold with at the end of episode 14.
[^7]: nazrānā: a levy taken from a village, presented as a tribute owed rather than as plunder. The word is kept because that distinction is the point of the passage and is what the Singhs are claiming.
