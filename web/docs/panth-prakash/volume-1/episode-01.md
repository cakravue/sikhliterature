# Panth Prakash, Episode 1
## The sakhi of the first origin of the Khalsa
### (panth khātar gur itī kamāī: so much did the Guru earn for the Panth)

*ik oaṅkār satigur prasādi.*\
One Being; realised by the grace of the True Guru.

*srī vāhigurū jī kī fatahi hai.*\
The victory is Sri Waheguru ji's.

_NOW BEGINS: SRI GUR PANTH PRAKASH GRANTH._

I bow at the feet of Sri Guru Nanak and hold Guru Gobind Singh in my mind. I am setting out to write how the Khalsa came into being; put out your own hand and help me through it.[^1]

Now I write the sākhī of the Singhs, in the shape our elders gave it when they spoke it to us, and in the shape I heard it from men older still, aged Sikhs of many virtues.[^2] I tell it also as far as my own understanding reaches, with mind and body set in love, in a devotion that burns fear away. I tell it as I told it to the English, and I set that account down here as the opening of the whole.[^3]

There had come to this country a French Firangi called Dhabai, whom the Marathas took into their service as a fighting man. They put one company in his charge, and he stayed their servant many years.[^4] Then he grew old, and his own country pulled at him, so he sold off everything he had built up and went home. Piru was his sister's son; to Piru he sold it and handed it all over before he left.[^5] Out of that one company Piru made four, and by force he held the whole stretch of Ganges country between Delhi and Agra.[^6]

The Marathas grew uneasy at this and wanted the country back out of his hands. So Piru settled on a mansūbā of his own: he sold the country to the English and went.[^7] In the year eighteen hundred and sixty (Sammat 1860 Bikrami, 1803 CE) he threw the Marathas away. The English held the country fast, and mourning settled on the house of the Marathas.[^8] Then the colonel came into Delhi and took possession, and Loni Akhtar was the general there. But the Singhs rode up and laid siege to Delhi, and the grip of the English went slack.[^9]

Anger rose in the English general's mind, and still no force of his would serve against the Singhs. So the English put a question to the Mughal Patshah: who was it who had given the Singhs a country?[^10] "What enemy have they ever killed? Whose business have they ever set right? Who had a patta written out for them? Name us that Shah."[^11]

*The Patshah of Delhi speaks.*

Then the Patshah gave his reply. "No Shah ever wrote anything over to them. They have set no one's business right, and they have killed no enemy of anyone's.[^12] It was Nijmal the Mughal who called in Nadir and had Delhi destroyed by him; it was Najib the Ruhela who summoned Ahmad Shah and by him had Bhau the Maratha brought down.[^13] Then Ahmad Shah wore himself out and turned back, and that is the only reason this country has stood in revolt against us." In this way the wicked Patshah spoke his deceit, and touched off the whole charge against the Singhs.[^14]

He called them rayyat and nothing more. He never let it be known that they were the Khalsa Panth. He said nothing of the mystery of the Gurus, nor of how the quarrel between the Gurus and the Shahs had run on.[^15] He did not tell how Sri Guru Nanak came, nor how his glory grew greater after him, nor how grace fell even upon Babur. He said none of it, keeping sin in his heart.[^16] He did not tell how the Turks then tormented the Hindus, nor how they harried the Sikhs and their Gurus, nor how the Gurus held to their resolve time after time: they gave their heads and did not give up their resolve.[^17] Nor how the enmity between the Shahs and the Guru came about; nor how the Panth was made at the Guru's command; nor how Chandi was awakened to help the Panth, and offerings laid before her, and his own sons given over to the work.[^18]

He offered up his own body to Chandi as well, and the evil of it came down on the heads of the Turks. So much did the Guru earn for the Panth, and not one word of it did that wicked man let the English hear.[^19]

The Guru's children were without sin and without crime, infants still at the milk. Guiltless, they were seized and their throats were cut, and of that too he gave no account.[^20]

Afterwards Bahadur Shah won the Guru over; the Guru made him Patshah and showed all his kalā in the doing of it. Then, by sending Banda, he took that Patshahi back again, and it came to the Tatt Khalsa.[^21] He told nothing of the Gurus and nothing of the Panth: how many years had gone by in that grappling between the Patshahs and the saintly Gurus.[^22]

Nor how Sikhs had themselves cut apart joint from joint, and their scalps lifted from their heads. Nor how stakes were planted in village upon village, until the strength of the Mughals gave out under it.[^23]

He did not tell the whole of Nadir either, who plundered Delhi and put its people to the sword; nor how the Singhs plundered Nadir in their turn, falling on his road home to Kandahar and Kabul and taking the loot back off him.[^24] Nor how Ahmad Shah afterwards seized Lahore and settled in it, and seized everything from Delhi to Agra; nor how the Singhs drove him out and took his subas from him by force.[^25] Nor how they sent his son Taimur out by the road of the dead, and how he went off having written out a talaq upon this land, that no child of his line should ever come this way again.[^26] After him came his grandsons, and the Khalsa beat them back as well. From its very beginning the Panth had come up grappling, and still the wicked Patshah called them rayyat.[^27]

A wicked man does not give up his wickedness, as a snake does not give up its venom. Give it milk as often as you please; it will not be turned aside from striking.[^28]

Not one true word did he speak. He lied, and as he lied he knew where the blow would fall. Whatever he said, the general set down in writing just so, and never weighed then which was the truth and which the lie.[^29] Between Turk and Hindu the enmity was there from the beginning, a quarrel of dīn and mazhab from the start. If a lie will kill your enemy, when was a Turk ever going to hold back from lying about a Hindu?[^30]

Wet the arm with oil and lay it down among sesame: as many seeds as cling to the arm, so many oaths a man may swear, and kill Hindus. This is what their rasūl left them as his word.[^31]

Rattan Singh has set down in writing what he heard, as he heard it. Listen, saints, with your minds fixed; from here I carry the account forward.[^32]

---
*Thus in the Sri Gur Panth Prakash, the first sakhi, of the origin of the Khalsa ji.*

### Notes

[^1]: misl: one of the confederacies into which the eighteenth-century Khalsa was organised. · Budha Dal: the senior of the Khalsa's two standing armies, the wing of the older Singhs. · Rattan Singh was born into the families he is about to write about. He was the grandson of Bhai Mehtab Singh, who cut off Massa Ranghar's head for the dishonouring of Harmandir Sahib and carried it before the Panth, and the son of Rai Singh; on his mother's side he was the daughter's son of Bhai Sham Singh of Narowal, of the Karorsinghia misl, who spent his whole life in the Budha Dal and was a sunehria of Baba Jassa Singh Ahluwalia, the fourth Jathedar of the Dal, meaning one of those who ate from a single bowl with him. In Baba Santa Singh Akali's steek this is why the book can be trusted: it is a family history, of the years when a Sikh head sold in Lahore for eighty rupees, and it had been carried breast to breast among the Gursikhs long before it was written down.

[^2]: sākhī: an account, a told story; the unit into which this book is divided, and what this project calls an episode. · The elders Rattan Singh means are named in Baba Santa Singh Akali's steek as Sham Singh, Mehtab Singh, his father Rai Singh and Sukha Singh, all of whom had kept company with older Singhs in their turn. · The steek also tells how Rai Singh survived. Mehtab Singh, after killing Massa Ranghar, left the boy with Nattha, the chaudhari of his village Mira Kot, and got away to Bikaner. When the Mughals demanded the child, Nattha decided he would rather answer for him in the court of God than hand him over, and fought until he and his companions were killed. Rai Singh, holding on to Nattha's clothes in the fighting, was struck across the neck and left for dead with the breath-vein intact; a woman selling greens found him still moving, carried him off in her basket to his aunt, and had the village cobbler stitch the wound. He lived, and stood in the front rank at Sirhind with Banda Bahadur, which is why he is remembered as a living martyr. · On the aged Sikhs of many virtues, the reading in the steek is that the Panth is itself the school in which such virtues are learned, so that men who come into it untaught come out wise.

[^3]: Baba Santa Singh Akali takes this literally: the English had the whole account written down, tested the true against the false in it, and sent copies out in three hundred and sixty languages, so that it still stands in royal libraries everywhere.

[^4]: Firangi: a European, from Frank. Rattan Singh's word for the French and the English alike. · Dhabai. Kulwant Singh: "Count Benoit De Boigne was a French general in the Maratha Army with its capital in Satara Poona."

[^5]: Piru. Kulwant Singh: "General Peitre Perron: A French seaman who landed in India in 1780, and became an officer under De Boigne in the Maratha (Scindia) Army. He took over the command of the Maratha Army after De Boigne. The Maratha Army under the Command of general Perron was badly defeated by General Lake in 1803."

[^6]:

[^7]: mansūbā: a scheme, a design; the Persian word of statecraft, and one of the many Rattan Singh reaches for when he writes about rule.

[^8]:

[^9]: Loni Akhtar is the poet's rendering of Ochterlony. Kulwant Singh: "Sir David Ochterlony, An American by descent, who was a commander of the British forces in Bengal. His forces under the overall command of Lord Lake defeated the Maratha Army under the command of Jaswant Rao Holkar and captured Delhi in 1804. Later on, he was the British representative at Delhi dealing with the British relations with the Sikh State of Maharaja Ranjit Singh." · The Sikh commander was Baghel Singh, and in Baba Santa Singh Akali's steek the Singhs plundered Delhi in broad daylight, with the gathering of Diwali drawing near.

[^10]: Patshah: a king, a sovereign; Shah is the same word short. Bhangu uses it of the Mughal emperor and, as Sikh usage does, of the Gurus. · Patshahi is the sovereignty itself.

[^11]: patta: the written royal grant by which a country was made over to a subject. The Patshah is being asked to name the sovereign whose patta the Singhs hold, which is the only ground on which the English would recognise a claim, and the question the whole book is written to answer.

[^12]:

[^13]: Kulwant Singh: "Nijmal Mughal, A leader of the Durrani faction in the court of Mansur Ali Nizamul-Mulk Mohammad Shah who invited Nadir Shah Durrani to plunder Delhi in 1739 A.D."; "Nadir: Nadir Shah Durrani, A Persian Invader from Afghanistan"; "Ruhela: Najib-ud-Doula, the chief of the Ruhela Sect of the Ruhelkhand Region, east of Delhi who invited Ahmad Shah Abdali to invade Delhi"; "Ahmad Shah: Ahmad Shah Abdali alias Durrani, son of Zaman Khan and the commander of Nadir Shah Durrani, who after the demise of Nadir Shah captured Kandhar, Balakh, Sind Punjab and Kashmir in 1747. He invaded Punjab eight times between 1747-1767. He desecrated and destroyed the Harmandir Sahib at Amritsar in 1761. He died in 1773." · Bhau is Sadashivrao Bhau, who commanded the Maratha army destroyed by Ahmad Shah Abdali at Panipat in 1761.

[^14]: To touch off the charge is toṛā jhāṛnā, to set light to the slow-match of a matchlock. The reading in Baba Santa Singh Akali's steek is that the Patshah aimed the English at the Singhs deliberately, to have their power broken so that they could not come back into Delhi.

[^15]: rayyat: the ruled, the common subjects, as against a Patshah; the whole quarrel of this episode turns on the word. · Baba Santa Singh Akali's instance of the quarrel the Patshah would not describe is Jahangir, who said that Guru Arjan Dev ji had opened a false shop and that he would shut it down.

[^16]: saṅgat: the gathered congregation. · The grace shown to Babur is a tradition the verse assumes its reader knows, and Baba Santa Singh Akali gives it in full: Guru Nanak granted Babur the Patshahi of Hindustan on condition that he never raise a hand against the Guru's house, warning that if he did it would be taken from him and given to the saṅgat. The blessing had been of seven Patshahis, but his line grew impatient, and it was taken back.

[^17]: Their resolve is sirṛa, the refusal to be moved. The Gurus meant are Guru Arjan, made to sit on the heated plate, and Guru Tegh Bahadur, beheaded at Delhi.

[^18]: yagya: a sacrificial rite performed at a fire. · Baba Santa Singh Akali reads Chandi as the power of Akal Purakh and not as a goddess apart from him: the formless one cannot be seen, only his power can, and it is that power which people name Bhavani or Kalka and which the Sikhs call Chandi. The awakening is the yagya performed on the hill of Naina Devi. He grounds the reading on two lines, one from Japji Sahib, *ekā māī jugat viāī tin chele parvāṇ; ik saṅsārī ik bhaṅḍārī ik lāe dībāṇ* (One mother conceived and gave birth, and three approved disciples were born: one the maker of the world, one its steward, one who holds the court), and one from the Bachittar Natak, *mahākāl kālkā arādhī ... dvai te ek rūp hvai gayo* (I worshipped Mahakal and Kalka ... from two I became one form). · For the Panth made at the Guru's command he points to the Bachittar Natak again, *hain apnā sut tohi nivājā, panth prachur karbe kahu sājā* (I have favoured you as my own son; I have fashioned you to spread the Panth).

[^19]: The offering of the Guru's own body is the wounding at Nanded, where the Pathans who struck him were the grandsons of Painde Khan. Baba Santa Singh Akali's reading is that a body is made pure by the touch of a weapon, so that the Guru's own death was itself the offering; he sets beside it Bhai Taru Singh's answer to the Singhs who asked to bathe him as he daily bathed them, that they should take the weapon in the field instead, for that and not his service was what would make their bodies pure.

[^20]: The children are the two younger Sahibzade, Baba Zorawar Singh and Baba Fateh Singh, put to death at Sirhind. In Baba Santa Singh Akali's account they were bricked into a foundation, and when the wall burst and their bodies fell out they were still living, and the qazi then ordered their throats cut.

[^21]: kalā: power, the working of a faculty. · Kulwant Singh: "Bahadur Shah I, (Prince Muazam) son of Aurangzeb whom Guru Gobind Singh helped to capture the Delhi throne in 1707, after his victory over his brother prince Alam Shah"; and of the Tatt Khalsa, "The mainstream Sikh faction of the Khalsa forces". · Baba Santa Singh Akali gives the means and the terms. Bahadur Shah won the Guru over through his munshi Bhai Nand Lal, who told him the only way was to go into the Guru's refuge and write to him himself, disowning his father's order against Anandpur. The Patshahi was granted on the condition that the men who had done the cruelties be punished, and when Bahadur Shah put that off, it was taken back and Banda sent in his place. Banda had asked to be made a Sikh and been told that becoming a Sikh is very hard, that the Gurus were themselves still becoming Sikhs, and he had then asked instead to be given a service.

[^22]:

[^23]: Bhai Mani Singh, granthi of Harmandir Sahib, was executed at Lahore and cut apart joint by joint in 1737. Bhai Taru Singh was executed at Lahore in 1745, his scalp cut from his head rather than let his hair be cut.

[^24]: Baba Santa Singh Akali adds what the Singhs took back off Nadir: he got away with far less than he came for, and the Khalsa recovered the goods of Hindustan from him down to the doors of Somnath, freed seventy thousand daughters out of the plunder, and then sent them home to every house at their own cost.

[^25]: suba: a province of the empire, and so also its governorship. It was on taking Lahore, in Baba Santa Singh Akali's account, that Ahmad Shah Abdali demolished Harmandir Sahib; the subas the Singhs took from him were Jalandhar, Lahore, Multan and Jammu.

[^26]: talaq: a divorce, in the legal sense, pronounced and written out. Taimur Shah was Ahmad Shah Abdali's son and his governor at Lahore, and the talaq, like the road of the dead, comes from what happened at Gujranwala. In Baba Santa Singh Akali's account Taimur marched from Lahore and ringed Charhat Singh in the town; a relieving force of Singhs struck from outside while Charhat Singh struck from within, and Taimur had no road left but flight. He lay all night among the dead in the road while the Singhs pursued as far as the Ravi, and came out and ran for Lahore only once they had turned back. What he wrote out as he went was that no child of his would come this way into Hindustan again.

[^30]: dīn and mazhab: faith and creed, the pair of Persian words in which the quarrel of religions was conducted in the courts of the time. The accent on dīn is there only to keep it apart from the English word.

[^31]: rasūl: the Prophet; Muhammad. · Baba Santa Singh Akali props the verse on a tradition of the court of God, in which Babur, sent there, finds Muhammad thrown out of it and asks him why, and is told it is for the teaching he had left his people: that the Hindus were kafirs, to be killed without question and without appeal. The answer given him is that the whole creation belongs to God, and that for this teaching he had forfeited his right to enter.
